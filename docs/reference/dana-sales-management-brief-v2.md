## I. Foundational Philosophy

### The Core Thesis

Sales management is the highest-leverage point in any revenue organization. A frontline manager with 8 reps can compound or destroy value at 8x the rate of any individual contributor. Yet most sales managers were promoted because they were great sellers, not because they were trained to lead — and the result is a measurable productivity tax on the entire organization.

My work is built on a simple premise: **sales management is a learnable discipline, not an innate trait.** With the right frameworks, the right operating rhythm, and the right AI infrastructure, ordinary managers can produce extraordinary results.

### Three Beliefs That Drive Everything

1. **Beneath metrics are behaviors.** You cannot coach numbers. You can only coach the activities and skills that produce numbers. Managers who chase the lagging indicator instead of the leading behavior will always be reactive.
2. **Salespeople are performers.** Like athletes, musicians, and surgeons, their results depend on execution in critical moments under pressure. They need structured practice, deliberate coaching, and a championship environment.
3. **Small incremental improvements compound.** A 0.5% improvement per week compounds to roughly 30% annually. A 10% lift across the seven key moments of a sales process can deliver hundreds of thousands in additional revenue without a single new hire.

### Why This Matters Now

AI is changing the economics of sales management. The administrative burden that used to consume 60–70% of a manager's week — pipeline hygiene, call scoring, 1:1 prep, deal review preparation, forecast triangulation — can now be automated. The question is no longer *whether* AI belongs in the manager's workflow, but *how to redirect the freed time toward the high-leverage activities that AI cannot do*: coaching humans, building culture, and making judgment calls under uncertainty.

## II. The Manager's Real Job: The Core Six Responsibilities

Most managers I meet are drowning. They get it from all directions: leadership pressure from above, rep needs from below, customer escalations from the side, and a calendar full of meetings that produce nothing. The first thing I do is help them clarify what their job actually is.

The Core Six are the only things a frontline sales manager should be spending meaningful time on:

1. **Opportunity Qualification** — Setting the bar for what constitutes a real deal and inspecting rigorously
2. **Deal Management** — Driving strategy, multi-threading, and removal of obstacles on live opportunities
3. **Call Planning & Coaching** — Pre-call preparation, post-call review, and skill development on the work itself
4. **Territory Management** — Account targeting, prioritization, and coverage
5. **Account Management** — Strategic management of named accounts and expansion
6. **Team Skill Development** — Building repeatable selling capability across the team

Everything else is either delegated, automated, or eliminated. The reframe matters: when a manager understands these are the only six things on their job description, the next conversation is about calendar control, not capacity.

### The Ideal Time Allocation

- Opportunity Qualification: 25–30%
- Deal Management: 20–25%
- Call Planning & Coaching: 20–25%
- Territory Management: 15–20%
- Account Management: 15–20%
- Team Skill Development: woven through coaching time

Most managers I diagnose are spending less than 10% on early-stage qualification — which is precisely backwards. Research consistently shows the top 25% of managers invest the majority of their coaching time at the front of the funnel, where compounding effects are highest.

## III. Behavior Change as the Manager's Mission

### The Reframe

A manager's job is not to "hit the number." A manager's job is to **change the behaviors that produce the number.** The number is downstream. The behaviors are upstream. If you are inspecting only the number, you are coaching the wrong thing.

### The Math of Incremental Improvement

This is the most important slide I show to skeptical managers:

- A team running 7 key moments at current performance produces a baseline number
- Improving each of those 7 moments by just 10% — not 50%, not 100%, just 10% — produces a compounded uplift in the multiple-hundreds-of-thousands of dollars range, depending on deal size
- Improvements of 0.5% per week, sustained for a year, equal roughly 30% annual improvement
- This is achievable through coaching alone, with no headcount additions, no comp changes, and no new tools

Once managers internalize this, the conversation about whether coaching is worth their time ends.

### Activity Coaching Beats Skill Coaching by 12x

This is the most important empirical finding in modern sales management research, and it is the one most managers get backwards.

Michelle Vazzana's *Crushing Quota* research found that **activity coaching drives approximately 24% of quota variance, while skill coaching drives only 2%.** Activity coaching has roughly twelve times the leverage. The reason is psychological: salespeople are motivated by **clarity of task** — the most consistently identified primary motivator in the research. "Make 30 calls today, run them through the cadence, and document outcomes" is concrete, executable, and trackable. "Get better at discovery" is abstract and demotivating because it gives the rep nothing to actually do.

The implication is that the highest-leverage coaching converts skill goals into activity targets. *"Improve your discovery"* becomes *"On every discovery call this week, ask the three Pain questions in this order, capture the customer's exact words, and we'll review on Friday."* The skill is built through the activity — not the other way around.

The corollary is that managers who spend most of their coaching time on advanced skill polish are leaving 12x leverage on the table. The team's lowest-performing reps almost always have an activity gap, not a skill gap.

### Motivational Interviewing in Sales Coaching

Drawing from clinical motivational interviewing principles — and frankly from my legal training in cross-examination — the most effective coaching conversations follow a five-step structure:

1. **Open with curiosity** — *"Help me understand…"* (not *"Why didn't you…"*)
2. **Share the data** — *"Here's what I'm seeing in the numbers…"*
3. **Diagnose together** — *"What do you think is happening?"*
4. **Co-create the solution** — *"What would help you improve?"*
5. **Commit to support** — *"Here's how I'll help."*

This is the difference between *being told* and *arriving at a conclusion*. Reps who arrive at a conclusion own it. Reps who are told resent it. (See Section IX for the full coaching protocol, including the deeper-probe layer used inside Steps 3-4 and the Reframe variant for when the rep's frame is wrong.)

### Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation

Comp plans, contests, and recognition (extrinsic) work in the short term but create dependence. Mastery, autonomy, and purpose (intrinsic) create sustainable performance. The manager's job is to know what drives each rep individually — and to connect their daily work to what matters to them personally. A manager who cannot answer "what intrinsically motivates each of my reps?" has homework to do.

## IV. The Three Principles (Secure Attachment Theory in Management)

Borrowed from developmental psychology — specifically John Bowlby's secure attachment theory — these three principles describe the relationship a high-performing manager builds with each rep:

1. **Availability** — Be there when needed. Reps know they can reach you with a problem and you will respond. They are not abandoned in the field.
2. **Non-interference** — *"Don't put on your cape."* Do not jump on the call and close the deal for them. Do not write the email for them. The temptation is enormous because you can do it faster and better — but every time you rescue them, you train them to need rescuing.
3. **Unconditional positive regard** — Support without judgment. The rep should feel safe bringing you a deal that's falling apart, a call that went sideways, or a personal issue. Psychological safety is the precondition for growth.

Reps with secure attachment to their manager admit mistakes, ask for help, share challenges, and push back on ideas. Reps without it hide problems until those problems become fires. If your team isn't doing the four behaviors above, that's diagnostic data about you, not them.

## V. The Champion's Code: Building the Environment for Performance

Behavior change requires two things: individual motivation (covered above) and the right environment. The Champion's Code is the environment.

The core analogy: **build sales teams like championship sports teams.** Athletes practice more than they compete. They watch film. They receive specialized coaching. They have rituals of celebration. They study opponents. Sales teams, by contrast, mostly just compete — and then wonder why they don't improve.

The seven components:

### 1. Fielding the Right Team

You cannot coach your way out of a hiring problem. The Champion's Code begins with disciplined recruiting against an Ideal Rep Profile, structured interviews, and the willingness to manage out reps who don't fit. The most expensive hire is the wrong one kept too long.

### 2. Know Your Numbers

Every rep on a championship team knows their stats — and so does every athlete. In sales, this means activity metrics, conversion rates, win rates, average deal size, sales cycle length, and forecast accuracy. Numbers are visible, discussed, and owned. Hiding from numbers is not allowed.

### 3. Culture of Coaching

Coaching is scheduled, not ad-hoc. Coaching is specialized (discovery vs. demo vs. negotiation). Coaching is two-way — peer coaching and manager coaching. The team expects coaching the way an athlete expects practice.

### 4. Culture of Practice

Role play is non-negotiable. Reps practice difficult moments before they happen — discovery questions, objection handling, executive conversations — until execution is unconscious. This is the muscle most sales teams skip, and it is precisely the muscle that separates pros from amateurs.

### 5. Culture of Collaboration

Wins are team wins. Losses are team losses. Reps share intel, hand off accounts cleanly, and celebrate each other's deals. This is the opposite of the lone-wolf cowboy culture that most sales orgs accidentally reward.

### 6. Culture of Continuous Learning

Win/loss reviews. Call libraries. Book clubs. External speakers. Industry conferences. The team gets visibly smarter every quarter — and each rep can name what they've learned.

### 7. Culture of Celebration

Wins are recognized publicly, specifically, and proportionately. Recognition is one of the cheapest performance levers in management, and it is the most underused.

## VI. The Operating Rhythm

Without a deliberate weekly and monthly cadence, managers default to firefighting and the Core Six gets crowded out. The operating rhythm protects the time.

### The Three Pillars of the Sales Operating System

Borrowing from Verne Harnish's *Mastering the Rockefeller Habits*, an effective operating rhythm rests on three interlocking pillars. All three must be present; missing any one collapses the system.

- **Priorities — the singular Quarterly Rock.** The entire team aligns behind one critical objective for the next 90 days. Not a list of ten priorities, not a balanced scorecard — *one*. This singular focus cascades from BHAG → Quarterly Rock → Monthly Theme → Weekly Priority → Daily Action, giving every rep a clear line of sight from their daily work to the team's most important goal.
- **Data — the antidote to opinion.** Information flows accurately and quickly. Every meeting is grounded in objective data from CRM and dashboards, not subjective stories. The guiding test: *every employee can answer quantitatively whether they had a good day or week.*
- **Rhythm — the heartbeat.** A predictable cadence of daily, weekly, monthly, and quarterly meetings creates a synchronized pulse. Rhythm is not "more meetings"; it is making every interaction purposeful and interconnected.

The Rockefeller insight: *"Routine sets you free."* A disciplined rhythm is not bureaucracy; it is the operating system that makes everything else executable.

### Weekly Cadence

**Monday Morning Team Meeting (45–60 min)**

- Results & metrics from prior week (10 min)
- Forecast updates from each rep (15 min)
- Weekly focus — skill of the week (10 min)
- Deal spotlight from one rotating rep (15 min)
- Coordination & planning (10 min)

This is *not* the venue for strategy, deep coaching, or storytelling. It sets the tone and the priorities for the week.

**Tuesday–Thursday: One-on-Ones**

The sacred 60-minute coaching session (see Section X).

**Friday Team Focus Session (alternating bi-weekly)**

- Week A: Deep Dive Deal Review (2 deals, 60 min)
- Week B: Skill Development Workshop (focused practice)

### The Diagnose & Treat Principle

This is the most important architectural principle in the operating rhythm: **group meetings diagnose; triggered 1:1s treat.**

Group meetings (the Monday team meeting, the Friday deal review, the QBR) surface team-level patterns. *"Our discovery-to-demo conversion has dropped 15%."* That is a diagnosis. The group meeting is the right place for it.

Triggered 1:1s (the weekly coaching session, an ad-hoc deal strategy session, a call coaching block) treat individual gaps. *"Sarah, let's work on what's happening in your discovery calls."* That is treatment. The triggered 1:1 is the right place for it.

The cardinal sin of sales management is attempting to solve individual performance problems in a group setting. It humiliates the individual, demotivates the team, and produces no behavior change. Use the group meeting to identify the *what* (the team-level pattern); use the targeted 1:1 to address the *who* and *how* (the individual rep and the specific skill or strategy).

### Three Distinct Types of Deal Reviews

This is one of the most important distinctions in my work, because most managers conflate all three and get the worst of all worlds.

| Type | Purpose | Question | Format |
|------|---------|----------|--------|
| **Forecast Call** | Predict the number | "How much will you sell this quarter?" | 30 min, numbers only, no storytelling |
| **Pipeline Scrub** | Validate data accuracy | "Is this data right?" | Embedded in 1:1s, focuses on values, dates, stages |
| **Deep Dive Deal Review** | Collaborative strategy | "How do we win this deal?" | 60 min, 2 deals, peer + leader input |

**Warning sign**: too much storytelling on a forecast call means it has degraded into a deal review. Stop and reset.

### From Pipeline Theater to Pipeline Surgery

The deepest pathology in most pipeline reviews is what I call **Pipeline Theater** — the manager goes around the room asking for updates, the rep provides a subjective optimistic summary, the manager says "sounds good" and moves on. Zero value is added. Risks are hidden by happy ears. The forecast becomes a work of fiction. The team is bored, and 30 minutes are wasted.

The alternative is **Pipeline Surgery** — an active, structured inspection of pre-selected deals using a qualification framework as a scalpel. The manager becomes a diagnostician, asking probing questions to stress-test deal health: *"What evidence do you have? How do we get to the Economic Buyer? What is our quantifiable value proposition?"* The output is a critical gap identified, a concrete action plan, and a forecast that becomes more accurate because it is based on reality.

The empirical case for Pipeline Surgery is overwhelming:

- **67% of deals in the "Proposal" stage have never had a confirmed, accessible budget**
- **73% of stalled deals are missing confirmed access to the Economic Buyer**
- **89% of deals lost to "no decision" lacked a quantified business case showing the cost of inaction**

Pipeline Theater allows these statistics to kill the forecast. Pipeline Surgery prevents it.

### Bi-Monthly Forecast Cadence (Not Weekly)

This is where I push back against industry orthodoxy. Weekly forecast calls produce diminishing returns because deals don't move stages every week — typical stage movement takes 12–15 days. Weekly forecast calls become storytelling exercises.

I recommend:

- **Beginning of month**: Comprehensive forecast review with full SPICED inspection
- **Mid-month**: Checkpoint to adjust based on movement and surface emerging risk

The freed weekly slots get reinvested in coaching and deal strategy.

### Quarterly Business Review (Half-Day)

- Performance assessment (60 min)
- Market & competitive review (45 min)
- Strategy adjustment (60 min)
- Team development planning (60 min)
- Next quarter planning (60 min)

## VII. Deal Management & Qualification

### SPICED Over MEDDPICC (For Most B2B Deals)

For the majority of B2B SaaS sales motions, I prefer SPICED over MEDDPICC. SPICED is more conversation-driven, more buyer-centric, and easier for reps to internalize. MEDDPICC remains appropriate for very complex enterprise deals with formal procurement processes, but most teams over-rotate on it.

**SPICED:**

- **S — Situation**: ICP fit, current state, trigger event
- **P — Pain**: Multiple specific pain points, prioritized by impact
- **I — Impact**: Quantified emotional + rational consequences, ROI
- **C — Critical Event**: Hard deadline with consequences if missed
- **E — Decision**: Process, criteria, committee, budget

Deals scoring 20+ on a 1–5 SPICED scale (across five elements) tend to close at 70%+ probability. Deals scoring under 12 are not deals — they're hopes.

### Stage-Specific Qualification Standards

| Stage | Minimum Required to Advance |
|-------|----------------------------|
| Lead/SQL | ICP fit confirmed, persona engaged, one pain acknowledged, discovery scheduled |
| SAL (post-demo) | Full SPICED documented, multiple stakeholders engaged, price range confirmed |
| SQL (proposal) | Value confirmed by prospect, ROI validated, budget + timeline confirmed, competition known |

Stage advancement should require manager validation, not rep self-attestation. Without exit criteria enforcement, every rep's pipeline becomes optimistic fiction.

### Three Things That Kill Deals (And How to Inspect for Them)

1. **No critical event** → Deal will slip indefinitely. Ask: *"What happens to them if this isn't solved by [date]?"* If the answer is "nothing material," there is no critical event.
2. **Single-threaded** → Deal dies if your champion changes roles. Ask: *"Who else have we engaged at director level or above?"* If the answer is "nobody," start multi-threading immediately.
3. **No quantified impact** → Deal can't survive procurement. Ask: *"What's the dollar value of solving this?"* If the rep can't answer, neither can the prospect — and procurement will compress price ruthlessly.

### Mutual Action Plans

For deals above ~$30K with multi-stakeholder approval, a Mutual Action Plan (MAP) is non-negotiable. A MAP is a shared timeline of milestones, owners, and dependencies — built collaboratively with the champion. It is positioned as implementation planning, not as a sales tactic. Done right, it accelerates deals 20–30% and surfaces risk early.

### Deal Review Structure (60 Minutes, 2 Deals)

1. Deal overview (5 min)
2. SPICED deep dive with evidence (15 min)
3. Win strategy: competitive position, multi-threading, timeline (10 min)
4. Action planning with owners and dates (5 min)

Repeat for second deal. Pre-work is mandatory: rep submits CRM-updated SPICED scorecard and call recordings 48 hours in advance.

## VIII. Forecasting Methodology

### The Triangulation Approach

A single forecasting method is always wrong. I teach managers to triangulate three independent estimates:

1. **Bottom-up** — Sum of individual rep commitments
2. **Stage-weighted pipeline** — Σ(Deal Amount × Stage-Specific Win Rate)
3. **Historical run-rate** — Trailing-quarter average adjusted for seasonality

When the three numbers agree within 10%, confidence is high. When they diverge, the gap *is* the forecast risk — and the conversation moves to investigating why.

### The Five-Step Deal Inspection Process

1. Collect rep forecasts with specific deal-level commitments
2. Compare against historical metrics (sales cycle, win rates by stage, rep accuracy history)
3. Inspect each forecasted opportunity against SPICED criteria
4. Adjust probabilities based on evidence (apply management judgment)
5. Consolidate, communicate, and track for accuracy

### Managing Forecast Pathologies

| Pathology | Symptom | Fix |
|-----------|---------|-----|
| **Hockey stick** | Massive end-of-quarter loading | Inspect early-stage rigor; build pipeline earlier |
| **Sandbagging** | Rep consistently beats forecast by 20%+ | Adjust personal probability multiplier |
| **Happy ears** | Rep consistently misses forecast | Mandatory evidence requirement for forecast inclusion |
| **Bluebirds** | Late-quarter unexpected deals | Discount appropriately; analyze for pipeline-building lessons |

### Target: 15–20% Improvement in Forecast Accuracy

This is the headline business outcome I commit to with new clients. It is achievable in a single quarter when SPICED is applied rigorously, the bi-monthly cadence is followed, and CRM hygiene is enforced.

## IX. Call Coaching Excellence

### The Five Principles of Effective Coaching

Drawn from research on what separates the top quartile of managers:

1. **75–80% of coaching time on early-stage opportunities** (where compounding is highest — Vazzana's research found top performers spend 78% of coaching time at the front of the funnel vs. 54% for average managers)
2. **Quality over quantity** — fewer, deeper sessions beat many shallow ones
3. **Scheduled over ad-hoc** — calendar-blocked, structured sessions beat reactive coaching. Research consistently shows high-performing managers *schedule* coaching sessions and conduct (rather than skip) them; cancellations are the most violated rule in the cadence and the one with the highest cost
4. **Process-driven over opinion-driven** — use objective scorecards, not subjective judgment
5. **Skills-based over deal-based** — coach the repeatable skill, not just "win this deal"

### TASK: The Diagnostic Framework

Before coaching, diagnose. The TASK framework forces specificity by descending from outcome to root cause in four steps, each independently coachable:

- **T — Target Result**: The lagging outcome being driven, paired with a specific drill-down metric. *"More pipeline"* is a wish; *"$600K pipeline per rep per quarter"* is a target. If it can't be reduced to a number, it isn't a target.
- **A — Activities (Key Sales Moments)**: The leading inputs that produce the target. This is where TASK integrates directly with the Seven Key Moments — every activity coaches to one of the seven moments (Prospecting & Qualification, Discovery, Demo, Solutioning, Business Case, Proposal & Negotiation, Close & Handoff) or a precursor activity that feeds into one. *"Calls, emails, LinkedIn engagement, prospecting cadences."*
- **S — Skill (Practiced and Quality)**: How the activity is performed. The two qualifiers matter: *practiced* (skill is built through deliberate repetition — Champion's Code Element 4), and *quality* (skill has a quality gradient — "made the calls" is activity, "ran the calls with strong objection handling, sharp value prop, and personalization" is skill).
- **K — Knowledge (Taught, Referenced, or Check-listed)**: What the rep needs to know to perform the skill, in three modes — taught (workshops, 1:1s), referenced (playbooks, battle cards), or check-listed (pre-call plans, discovery scripts). For most B2B contexts, the knowledge layer breaks down into the **3 P's**: Process knowledge, Product knowledge, and Prospect knowledge.

The diagnostic question at each level:

1. *Is the target clear and measured?* If no, fix that first.
2. *Are the activities being run at sufficient volume?* If no, the gap is activity, not skill.
3. *Are the activities being run with quality?* If no, the gap is skill — schedule practice.
4. *Does the rep know what they need to know?* If no, the gap is knowledge — teach, reference, or check-list.

A coaching session without a clear TASK diagnosis is a conversation, not coaching.

(TASK supersedes the REKS framework referenced in earlier versions. The change matters because Activities = Key Sales Moments creates an explicit bridge to the Rep Layer; the causal flow Target → Activity → Skill → Knowledge matches how managers actually diagnose; and the 3 P's of Knowledge integrate cleanly with enablement asset design. See `task-coaching-diagnostic.md` and `managing-to-metrics-library.md` for the full operational treatment.)

### "If This, Then That" Diagnostic Logic

Standard diagnostics save managers from reinventing the wheel:

- If connect rate is low → check time of day, list quality, opening hook
- If conversion is low → review opening framing, value proposition delivery
- If progression is low → audit discovery quality, SPICED completion
- If close rate is low → inspect critical event, decision committee mapping

### The 30–45 Minute High-Impact Coaching Session

1. Recap previous week's commitments (5 min)
2. Call review #1 with scorecard (10 min)
3. Role play to reinforce learning (5 min)
4. Call review #2 with scorecard (10 min)
5. Role play (5 min)
6. Wrap-up & action plan (5 min)

Two calls reviewed, two role plays per session. Most managers do zero role play. This is malpractice.

### The Five-Step Coaching Conversation: Two Layers

The five-step structure introduced in Section III is the **macro** structure for delivering observation-based feedback when the manager initiates the conversation based on data. There is also a **micro** questioning sequence for working through challenges the rep brings — a deeper-probe layer used inside Steps 3 and 4 of the macro.

The micro sequence — sometimes called the **Autonomy & Mastery protocol** — is five questions:

1. *"Tell me what's going on… what else?"* — let the rep articulate the full problem; mirror back what's heard
2. *"In an ideal situation, what would this look like?"* — shift from problem-state to solution-state; force visualization
3. *"What changes can you make to get there?"* — rep proposes the plan; manager refrains from prescribing
4. *"What will make this difficult for you?"* — surface the friction that will threaten the plan
5. *"What belief do you need to adjust to make this work?"* — name the underlying assumption beneath the obstacle

Step 5 is the one most managers skip. The obstacle a rep names in step 4 (*"I don't have time," "the customer won't engage"*) is rarely the real obstacle. The real obstacle is almost always a belief — *"I'll annoy the customer if I push harder. The deal will blow up if I ask about budget. Senior reps would think this question is basic."* Surfacing that belief and letting the rep examine it is what makes the change durable.

Two underlying principles inform why this works. **Process beats outcome**: the rep cannot control whether the customer says yes; they can only control the process they run. A coaching protocol focused on belief and behavior gets ownership of the only thing the rep can actually change. And **the mind rejects ideas it did not generate**: a belief shifted through the rep's own examination is durable; a belief told to the rep is dismissed within days.

### When to Use the Reframe

Occasionally a rep arrives at Step 2 or Step 3 with the wrong problem entirely. They believe they have a closing problem when they actually have a discovery problem. The five-step structure has a variation for this — the **reframe**, drawn from Challenger sales methodology:

1. **Validate the stated problem** — *"I understand why you're focused on closing technique."*
2. **Introduce the larger problem** — *"What we're seeing across similar deals is that closing struggles are usually a symptom of incomplete pain quantification two stages earlier."*
3. **Quantify the hidden cost** — *"The cost of fixing closing technique on a deal where pain wasn't quantified is roughly zero — the deal is already lost; you just don't know it yet."*
4. **Path to value** — *"Let me show you what we're going to work on instead, and the closing piece becomes much easier."*

The reframe is the manager's intervention when guided self-discovery is leading the rep toward the wrong destination. Use it sparingly — overuse is just prescription wearing a clever costume.

### Specific Positive Reinforcement

Within twenty-four hours of every coaching session, the manager sends a written summary that captures the agreements, the action items, and **one specific positive observation** about what the rep did well in the session.

Specificity is the entire game. *"Good job today"* is noise — even children stop responding to generic praise. *"I was struck by how quickly you identified the assumption about budget timing — that's a real shift from where you were three weeks ago"* is reinforcement. The first activates intrinsic motivation; the second does not.

One specific positive observation per session, every session, is the discipline. It costs nothing, takes 60 seconds to write, and is the single highest-leverage behavior compounder in the manager's toolkit.

### Pre-Call Planning Discipline

Every important call gets a written plan: research summary, primary objective, secondary objectives, minimum acceptable outcome, planned SPICED questions, anticipated objections, and ideal next step. The manager's job in coaching is to inspect the plan *before* the call, not to debrief after.

## X. The Sacred 60-Minute One-on-One

### Why 30 Minutes Isn't Enough

Industry data suggests 89% of 1:1s are 30 minutes or less, and 68% lack any consistent structure. This is the single highest-ROI hour in a manager's week, and it's being wasted on status updates.

### The Six Objectives of the 1:1

A 1:1 is built around six distinct purposes. A meeting that hits fewer than four of these in a given week has degraded into a status update.

1. Provide coaching and call planning for the seven Key Moments
2. Manage to the rep's performance goals (TASK-level metrics)
3. Inspect deals to refine forecast (per-deal SPICED)
4. Enforce CRM hygiene ("source of truth" discipline)
5. Identify and remove hurdles and blockers
6. Understand rep career goals

Note that "tell the rep what to do" is not on this list. The 1:1 is a coaching session, not a directive forum.

### The 60-Minute Structure

1. **Personal check-in** (5 min) — relationship and rapport
2. **Performance review** (10 min) — results, activity metrics, leading indicators
3. **Pipeline scrub & deal inspection** (15 min) — data validation + 2–3 key deals
4. **Skill development & call coaching** (20 min) — recording review, role play
5. **Career development** (5 min) — long-term goals, growth opportunities
6. **Action planning** (5 min) — specific commitments with timelines

### Monthly Topic Rotation

- **Week 1**: Territory and account planning
- **Week 2**: Pipeline management and qualification
- **Week 3**: Skill development and call coaching
- **Week 4**: Performance review and career development

This rotation prevents 1:1s from becoming groundhog-day pipeline reviews and ensures every dimension of the rep's development gets attention monthly. The rotation also operates inside a quarterly emphasis overlay: in Month 1 of a quarter, account/territory plan-setting and early-stage qualification dominate; in Month 3, late-stage pipeline review and closing technique dominate. Same rotation, different operational urgency.

### Pre-Work Requirements

**Manager** (before each 1:1):

- Review CRM, performance dashboard, recent call recordings
- Identify 2–3 deals requiring inspection (start with newest opportunities to ensure they were qualified well)
- Prepare coaching focus area for the session

**Rep** (48 hours before):

- Update all CRM data completely
- Submit self-assessment on focus skill
- Identify deals where they need support
- Document questions and challenges

If either side comes unprepared, the meeting is rescheduled. The discipline trains the discipline.

### The 1:1 Is Sacred — Cancellation Protocol

The 1:1 is **never cancelled, only rescheduled within the same week.** Cancellation signals "this isn't important," which destroys the entire coaching system over time. If the manager has a true conflict, the manager — not the rep — owns rebooking the slot. Three cancellations in a quarter, and the rep has internalized that coaching is the thing that gets cut when something else comes up. This is the most violated rule in the cadence and the one with the highest cost when violated.

## XI. AI Integration: The New Layer

### The Operating Principle

**AI amplifies human coaching; it does not replace it.** The reps still need to be coached by a human who cares about them. But the manager can now do that coaching with 10x the data, 10x the consistency, and a fraction of the prep time.

### The Eight Core Bots

These are the tools I'm building into client implementations:

1. **Management Analytics Bot** — Performance tracking, leading indicator dashboards, anomaly detection
2. **1:1 Prep Bot** — Compiles weekly snapshot per rep, surfaces top 3 coaching opportunities, tracks last week's commitments
3. **Call Scoring Bot** — 100% call coverage with objective SPICED scorecards (vs. the 5–10% sampling humans can do)
4. **SPICED Scoring Bot** — Real-time qualification scoring with evidence collection
5. **Deal Confidence Bot** — Predictive pipeline scoring, similar-deal pattern matching, risk identification

### SPICED

- **S**ituation, **P**ain, **I**mpact, **C**ritical Event, **D**ecision

- **T**arget Result, **A**ctivities (Key Sales Moments), **S**kill (Practiced and Quality), **K**nowledge (Taught, Referenced, or Check-listed)

### Three Types of Deal Reviews

1. Forecast Calls (numbers)
2. Pipeline Scrubs (data)
3. Deep Dive Deal Reviews (strategy)

### Pipeline Theater vs. Pipeline Surgery

- Theater: passive reporting, optimistic forecasts, zero new information
- Surgery: structured inspection of pre-selected deals using a qualification framework as a scalpel
- The 67/73/89 statistics: % of "Proposal" deals lacking budget / stalled deals lacking EB access / "no decision" losses lacking quantified business case

### The Five-Step Coaching Conversation (Macro)

1. Open with curiosity
2. Share the data
3. Diagnose together
4. Co-create the solution
5. Commit to support

### The Autonomy & Mastery Deeper Probe (Micro — used inside Steps 3-4)

1. Tell me what's going on… what else?
2. In an ideal situation, what would this look like?
3. What changes can you make to get there?
4. What will make this difficult for you?
5. What belief do you need to adjust to make this work?

### The Reframe Choreography (Challenger variant)

1. Validate the stated problem
2. Introduce the larger problem
3. Quantify the hidden cost
4. Path to value
