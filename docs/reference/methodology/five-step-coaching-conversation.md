# The Five-Step Coaching Conversation

> Methodology reference. Loaded by `manager/one-on-one-prep`, `manager/development-plan-generator`, `manager/friday-deal-review`, `manager/friday-skill-workshop`, and any Manager Layer skill that produces coaching conversation prompts.

## When to use this

Any time a manager delivers feedback, coaches a skill, addresses a performance gap, or works through a deal challenge with a rep. The structure applies whether the conversation is 5 minutes (impromptu in a hallway) or 25 minutes (the skill-development block of a 60-minute 1:1).

## The core thesis

The most effective coaching conversations are not directive. They are guided self-discovery.

The instinct of most managers — especially great-seller-promoted-to-manager managers — is to tell the rep what's wrong and what to do. The rep nods, leaves the room, and changes nothing. Reps who are *told* what to do resent it; reps who *arrive at the conclusion themselves* own it.

This is the central insight of clinical motivational interviewing (Miller & Rollnick) — and it transfers directly to sales coaching. A coaching conversation that produces durable behavior change follows a five-step structure that moves the rep from observation to commitment, with the manager as the curious partner rather than the directive instructor.

## The five steps

```
1. Open with curiosity
   ↓
2. Share the data
   ↓
3. Diagnose together
   ↓
4. Co-create the solution
   ↓
5. Commit to support
```

### Step 1: Open with curiosity

**The move:** Start with a question that invites the rep's perspective before any feedback is delivered.

**Language:**
- ✅ "Help me understand what happened on the Acme call."
- ✅ "Walk me through how you're thinking about this deal."
- ✅ "What did you notice about the discovery section?"
- ❌ "Why didn't you ask about budget?"
- ❌ "I noticed you missed a few SPICED elements."
- ❌ "We need to talk about your pipeline."

The "why didn't you" formulation puts the rep on the defensive immediately. The brain's threat response kicks in, and the rest of the conversation is processed through that lens. The "help me understand" formulation invites the rep into the diagnostic process as a partner.

This step is not optional and not something to rush. Five seconds of curiosity changes the entire conversation that follows.

### Step 2: Share the data

**The move:** Present the objective evidence — call recordings, scorecards, CRM data, pipeline metrics. Not the manager's interpretation; the data.

**Language:**
- ✅ "Here's what I'm seeing in the numbers — your discovery-to-demo conversion has dropped from 60% to 35% over the last six weeks."
- ✅ "Looking at the call transcript, the prospect mentioned cost three times in the first five minutes."
- ✅ "The SPICED scorecard shows Pain at 4 but Critical Event at 1."
- ❌ "Your discovery feels weak lately."
- ❌ "I think you're talking too much."

The discipline is to share *data*, not *judgment*. Data invites interpretation; judgment invites defense. If the manager doesn't have data, the manager isn't ready to coach yet — that's the signal to pull recordings, run the call scoring bot, or look at the dashboard before the conversation.

This is also where the **TASK diagnostic** does its work. The data points directly to which TASK level the gap is in (Target, Activity, Skill, or Knowledge), which makes the next step concrete.

### Step 3: Diagnose together

**The move:** Ask the rep what they see in the data. Resist the urge to explain.

**Language:**
- ✅ "What do you think is happening here?"
- ✅ "When you look at this, what stands out to you?"
- ✅ "What's your read on why the conversion dropped?"
- ❌ "So what I think is happening is…"
- ❌ "The reason you're missing critical events is…"

This is the step most managers skip. The manager has the answer in their head and wants to deliver it. But a rep who diagnoses the problem themselves owns the solution; a rep who has the diagnosis delivered to them is a passive recipient.

Reps usually arrive at roughly the right diagnosis with a few clarifying questions. The 10-15% of cases where they don't are also useful — they reveal a knowledge gap (TASK level K) that becomes part of the development plan.

### The deeper probe: Autonomy & Mastery questioning

Inside Steps 3 and 4, the manager often needs a more granular questioning sequence to guide the rep from diagnosis to a plan they own. The Autonomy & Mastery protocol is that sequence — five questions that move the rep through self-directed problem-solving rather than receiving a manager-prescribed solution.

The protocol applies whenever a rep is *working through a challenge they can articulate themselves* (not just receiving observation-based feedback). It is especially valuable when the rep brings the topic — "I've been struggling to get to the economic buyer in this segment" — rather than the manager bringing observed data.

| # | Question | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Tell me what's going on… what else?" | Lets the rep articulate the full problem and mirrors back what's heard |
| 2 | "In an ideal situation, what would this look like?" | Shifts from problem-state to solution-state; forces the rep to visualize the destination |
| 3 | "What changes can you make to get there?" | Rep proposes the plan; manager refrains from prescribing |
| 4 | "What will make this difficult for you?" | Surfaces the friction that will threaten the plan in the workweek |
| 5 | "What belief do you need to adjust to make this work?" | Names the underlying assumption beneath the obstacle |

Step 5 is the most important and the one most managers skip. The obstacle a rep names in step 4 ("I don't have time," "the customer won't engage") is rarely the real obstacle. The real obstacle is almost always a belief: *"I'll annoy the customer if I push harder. Senior reps would think this question is basic. The deal will blow up if I ask about budget."* Surfacing that belief and letting the rep examine it is what makes the change durable. Solving the surface obstacle without examining the belief produces three weeks of compliance and a relapse.

For interpersonal-conflict scenarios, an additional question fits at step 5: *"How would the other person see this?"* Forcing the rep into the counterparty's perspective often dissolves the belief faster than direct challenge.

Two underlying principles inform why this works:

- **Process beats outcome.** The rep cannot control whether the customer says yes; the rep can only control the process they run. A coaching protocol that focuses on belief and behavior rather than on results gets ownership of the only thing the rep can actually change.
- **The mind rejects ideas it did not generate.** A belief shifted through the rep's own examination is durable. A belief told to the rep by the manager is dismissed within days.

The protocol is a cheat sheet — managers should keep the five questions visible until they are reflexive. Mastery comes through repetition. The first ten times feel mechanical; by the fiftieth, the manager runs them without thinking.

### Step 4: Co-create the solution

**The move:** Ask the rep what they think would help — then add precision and specificity to their answer.

**Language:**
- ✅ "What would help you improve this?"
- ✅ "What's a different approach you'd want to try?"
- ✅ "Of those options, which feels most actionable for next week?"
- ❌ "Here's what you should do…"
- ❌ "I want you to do these three things…"

The rep proposes; the manager refines. The manager's role is to add prescriptive specificity to a directionally correct rep proposal. If the rep says "I should ask better discovery questions," the manager helps convert that into "Run the SPICED Pain question with this exact phrasing on your next three discovery calls and we'll review the recordings on Friday."

The solution is not the manager's idea — it is the rep's idea, sharpened. That distinction is what produces ownership.

### Step 5: Commit to support

**The move:** Close with what the manager will do, not just what the rep will do. Make the support specific and time-bound.

**Language:**
- ✅ "I'll send you the SPICED Pain reference card by end of day. We'll review the next three calls together on Friday at 2pm."
- ✅ "I'll set up a 30-minute role-play block next Tuesday so you can practice the new opening before your Wednesday demo."
- ✅ "Let's check in on this in our 1:1 next week — bring two recordings and your self-assessment."
- ❌ "Let me know how it goes."
- ❌ "Reach out if you need anything."

This step demonstrates the **availability** principle from secure attachment theory. The rep is not abandoned with the commitment. The manager has made an explicit, calendar-able promise to support the work.

## Why this structure works

Three psychological mechanisms:

1. **Curiosity → safety.** Step 1 disarms the threat response and creates the psychological safety required for honest reflection.
2. **Data → ownership.** Steps 2–3 give the rep evidence to interpret rather than judgment to receive. Reps who interpret evidence own the conclusion; reps who receive judgment resent it.
3. **Co-creation → commitment.** Steps 4–5 make the rep an architect of the solution rather than a recipient of an instruction. Architects build; recipients comply.

These mechanisms compound. A team coached this way reports performance challenges to the manager *earlier* (because they trust the response), proposes their own solutions *more often* (because they've practiced the pattern), and *retains skills longer* (because they own them).

## Common failure modes

| Failure mode | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Skipping Step 1 | "Let's talk about your pipeline" with no preamble | Always open with a curiosity question, even briefly |
| Confusing data with judgment in Step 2 | "Your discovery is weak" instead of showing the recording | If you can't show the data, you're not ready to coach |
| Pre-loading the diagnosis in Step 3 | "What I think is happening is X — do you agree?" | Ask the open question; tolerate the silence |
| Manager-driven solution in Step 4 | "Here's what I want you to do" | Let the rep propose; refine their proposal |
| Vague support in Step 5 | "Let me know how it goes" | Calendar-specific, evidence-based check-in |
| Rushing the structure | All five steps in 90 seconds | The five steps need 5–25 minutes depending on the depth of issue |

## Length and pace

The structure scales:

- **Quick coaching moment (5 min):** Each step is 1 minute. Useful for a single-call observation.
- **Standard 1:1 coaching block (20 min):** Steps 1–2 take 5 min, Step 3 takes 5 min, Step 4 takes 7 min, Step 5 takes 3 min.
- **Deep skill conversation (45 min):** Steps 1–2 take 10 min, Step 3 takes 15 min, Step 4 takes 15 min, Step 5 takes 5 min.

The mistake to avoid is collapsing the structure under time pressure. If only 5 minutes are available, run all five steps in 5 minutes. Do not run three steps well and skip the last two.
